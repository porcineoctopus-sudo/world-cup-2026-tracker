How the model works — where each team's strength comes from

The model rates only how far a team that has qualified goes, on a 0–5 ladder (group exit → champion). It splits each team's odds into two questions:

  • Reaching the last 16. Almost all the gap between teams lives here, driven by recent form (older tournaments count for less) and experience.
  • How far beyond that. Once a team is in the knockout, history barely predicts depth — the model is deliberately humble; deep runs are close to a coin-toss.

So France rates highly mainly because it is likely to reach the knockout, not because it is favoured once there. Croatia rates well on a thin record because recency rewards being strong lately. A minnow's title chance is tiny but not zero: it rarely clears the first hurdle, yet its depth odds look ordinary if it does.

It uses only each team's past tournament finishes — no squads, coaches, or rankings. The simplicity is the point: it barely beats a coin on knockout depth, and says so. Disagree with a rating? The sliders above are yours.

The data it learns from

Every World Cup from 1930 to 2022 — 22 tournaments, 354 knockout appearances across 65 nations. For each, one fact: how far the team got on the 0–5 ladder. No scores, squads, hosts, or rankings. It is a small dataset, which is a big reason the model stays simple, and it is tested walk-forward — trained only on tournaments before the one it predicts — so it never sees the answer it is graded on.

The features it looks at

From each team's past finishes it derives a handful of signals: recent form (each tournament back counts about 0.6× as much as the one after it), how often and how recently the team has reached the knockout, its trend, its best run, and how long since it last featured. These signals overlap heavily, so in practice a single recency-weighted form number does most of the work.

The two parts: the gate and the depth

Every team's odds are built from two questions. The gate — will it reach the last 16? — is where almost all the difference between teams sits; the chance of missing depends partly on field size (48 teams for only 16 second-round places), so a bigger tournament lengthens everyone's odds. The depth — once there, how far? — runs over the fixed 8/4/2/1/1 shape of the knockout, and here past record barely helps. Combining the two gives a full spread from 0 (out in the group) to 5 (champion).

How a single “strength” number arises

A team's strength summarises that 0–5 spread in one number: its average finish if the tournament were replayed many times — not a forecast of one result. Because even the best teams usually do not win, that average sits low: around 2 for France this year, far below the 5-point maximum, and near 0 for a debutant. So a side that reaches the semi-finals (worth 3) has not “beaten” its strength — it has simply landed above its average this time, as some team always does. This one number is what the sliders let you override, and what drives every match prediction.

How one match is predicted (head-to-heads)

The bracket and the simulation need something the finish model does not directly produce: the odds of one team beating another. This match layer is a separate, lighter-touch step — a sensible convention rather than something vetted against history like the strengths themselves. The bigger a team's strength edge, the more it is favoured; scorelines are drawn around that, and group ties are settled by the real FIFA rules — head-to-head first, then goal difference and goals — not a coin flip. Because it only sets how strongly the favourite is favoured, not who the favourite is, its rougher edges have limited reach.

How well it works — and what it can't see

Against a no-skill baseline — everyone gets the average odds — it is a clear but honest improvement, almost all of it from getting the gate right; on knockout depth it barely beats a coin, by design. Its blind spots are the things a finish-only record cannot see: the current squad and key players, injuries, the coach, home advantage, form in continental tournaments like the Euros and Copa América — and plenty more. The ratings are also draw-blind, though the app applies them through the actual group draw and bracket, so what you see already accounts for who each team must face. When you disagree, the sliders above are the honest fix — and the Prediction score above keeps a running tally of how your strengths and the model's each fare as real results come in.
